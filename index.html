Market Analysis Tool

A Python tool that pulls live market data across a personal watchlist and flags statistically unusual activity — volume spikes exceeding 2x the 30-day average, and price moves exceeding 1.5x the 20-day average true range. The watchlist itself is deliberate: NVDA and SPY are the names I trade options on most, TSLA and QQQ add a second high-beta name and a second broad benchmark so I can tell whether a move is stock-specific or market-wide, and AAPL, GOOG, and AMZN round it out with liquid, easy-to-read names. Built to support a personal options trading practice, with a modular architecture (data fetch, signal calculation, and reporting kept as separate components) designed for straightforward extension — implied volatility vs. historical volatility divergence is next.

How this was built

  • Backtested the existing volume/ATR flags against six months of history before adding IV/HV, to get an honest read on whether flagging predicts anything before layering on a third signal.
  • 1.5x ATR multiplier for the price-move signal — 2x barely fired (too quiet to be useful daily), 1.0x fired too often to mean anything; 1.5x was the middle ground.
  • IV/HV fetch failures are isolated from the main signal record, so one bad options-chain fetch doesn't blank out the volume/ATR numbers that already succeeded for that ticker.
  • Catalyst matching requires the ticker/company name in the headline title itself — Yahoo's news feed mixes in unrelated companies, so "closest in time" alone isn't enough to call something a catalyst.

Fixed Income / Yield Curve Analyzer

A Python tool that pulls the U.S. Treasury's daily par yield curve directly — no API key, just their public CSV — and tracks the 2Y-10Y and 3M-10Y spreads for inversion, alongside an interactive duration/convexity calculator built on the standard bond-math formulas. Same architecture as the market analysis tool: a pure-calculation module doing the spread math, a fetch layer doing the network I/O, and nothing in between mixing the two.

Research

04
Honours Thesis · December 2025

Returns to Education in Canada: An Econometric Analysis of Real Wages, 2018–2025

Carleton University, Department of Economics

Using pooled Labour Force Survey (LFS) microdata across 1,759,743 observations (weighted to ~558M person-records), I estimate a fully saturated Mincer-style wage model — ln(wi) = β₁Educationi + X′iγ + δp + δt + εi — controlling for occupation, industry, union status, establishment size, family structure, province, and year, with standard errors clustered at the province level. The result: education returns in Canada are strong, monotonic, and economically large even after stripping out every observable confounder I could get my hands on.

Other findings

  • Women earn ~15.7% less than men after controlling for education, occupation, industry, region, and job characteristics — a persistent, unexplained gap consistent with prior Statistics Canada research.
  • Regional premiums are large even after conditioning on productivity-linked covariates: Alberta (+12.4%) and British Columbia (+11.1%) pay a premium; Atlantic provinces carry a −5% to −7% penalty.
  • Kernel density estimates show the wage distribution for bachelor's-degree holders isn't just shifted right of the 0–8-years group — it's also more dispersed, with a materially thicker upper tail.

Methodology & limitations

  • Standard errors are clustered by province, not just heteroskedasticity-robust — wage determination correlates within a province via local labour-market and policy conditions, and ignoring that would understate the true uncertainty on every estimate.
  • Controls span occupation, industry, union status, establishment size, family structure, province, and year — the aim is isolating the education effect from as much observable confounding as the LFS microdata supports, not a crude raw comparison.
  • Limitation: this is observational, not causal. The model can't rule out that part of the education premium reflects unobserved ability or motivation rather than education itself — the standard ability-bias critique of Mincer-style wage regressions.
  • Limitation: LFS PUMF is a repeated cross-section, not a panel, so this captures a point-in-time association across different people, not within-person wage growth from gaining more education.
